\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves a language model's output by conditioning it on passages retrieved from an external corpus. Most of this work assumes a single, roughly homogeneous document collection. Practical knowledgebases violate that assumption: websites, PDFs, video transcripts, and code repositories are extracted, chunked, cited, and queried in different ways, and one query may need to draw on several of them at once. This survey organizes the techniques required for grounded question answering over such heterogeneous, multi-source knowledgebases. It covers dense retrieval and RAG foundations, hybrid lexical-dense reranking, chunking strategies for long and structured documents, modality-specific retrieval for web, video, and code, multi-document fusion, and confidence estimation with abstention. Instead of treating each technique in isolation, we examine how each one is affected once a corpus is assembled from structurally different sources at query time. One system, SAGE, which ingests and answers questions over crawled websites, PDFs, video transcripts, and GitHub repositories through a shared pipeline, appears as a brief case study showing how the surveyed ideas instantiate in a working implementation. We close with open problems in cross-modal fusion, confidence calibration, incremental re-indexing, and evaluation that remain unresolved across the surveyed literature.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become the dominant pattern for grounding language-model output in an external corpus. A retriever selects passages relevant to a question, and a generator produces an answer conditioned on those passages rather than on parametric memory alone \cite{lewis2020rag}. Since its introduction, RAG research has expanded rapidly along several axes, including retriever architecture, passage fusion, iterative retrieval, and calibration. Nearly all of this progress has been made and measured on a single, relatively uniform corpus, such as a Wikipedia dump, a set of internal wiki pages, or a collection of PDF manuals. A uniform corpus is analytically convenient, since it fixes one chunking strategy, one citation format, and one notion of a document.

That convenience disappears once the material a user wants to query spans structurally unrelated sources: web pages, formatted documents, spoken-language transcripts, and source code, each with its own extraction process, natural retrieval unit, and citation semantics. Answering a single query against whichever of these sources holds the relevant evidence, and returning a citation that identifies both the source and the location within it, requires composing techniques that were largely designed and evaluated one modality at a time. This composition problem, rather than any single retrieval or generation advance, is what motivates a survey dedicated to heterogeneous knowledgebases.

The survey is organized around that composition. Section~\ref{sec:foundations} reviews the retrieval and generation foundations common to every RAG system. Section~\ref{sec:reranking} turns to reranking and hybrid lexical-dense retrieval, whose role grows once a candidate pool may be drawn from sources with different lexical characteristics. Section~\ref{sec:chunking} examines chunking, where the appropriate retrieval unit for a code file, a video transcript, and a prose document rarely coincides. Section~\ref{sec:modality} surveys the modality-specific retrieval literatures for web documents, video and transcript QA, code-repository-aware retrieval, and multi-document fusion. Section~\ref{sec:confidence} addresses confidence estimation and abstention, which acquire an added dimension when a system must judge not only \emph{what} to retrieve but whether it retrieved \emph{enough} to answer responsibly across sources of differing quality. Section~\ref{sec:casestudy} then draws on one system, SAGE, to show how these pieces combine in a working pipeline; its source-overview chunk mechanism and its diversity floor over reranked results serve as worked examples of ideas introduced earlier, and not as the survey's subject. Section~\ref{sec:challenges} collects open problems that recur across the surveyed work, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\textbf{Scope and relation to prior surveys.} General RAG design, covering what to retrieve, when, how many passages, and how to fuse and iterate, is already surveyed at length \cite{gao2023ragsurvey}, and hallucination in generation has its own dedicated survey \cite{ji2023hallucination}. We duplicate neither. The angle taken here is narrower and complementary. Instead of surveying RAG technique by technique over an assumed-uniform corpus, we organize technique choices around the points of strain that emerge once a corpus is assembled from structurally different sources at query time. Chunking, reranking, and confidence calibration in particular are reconsidered in Sections~\ref{sec:reranking}--\ref{sec:confidence} because the single-corpus assumption they are usually described under no longer holds. The literature reviewed below was selected by beginning from the four modality-specific retrieval areas of Section~\ref{sec:modality} (web, video/transcript, code, and multi-document) and working back to the shared retrieval, reranking, chunking, and confidence machinery each assumes, rather than from any single venue's or year's proceedings. We claim no exhaustive coverage of any one of these four areas on its own terms.

\section{Problem Definition and Terminology}
\label{sec:problem}

Before surveying techniques, it is worth stating precisely what changes when a RAG system moves from one corpus to several heterogeneous ones, since much of the terminology in the RAG literature implicitly assumes a single corpus and does not distinguish these concerns.

We use \emph{source} to mean one ingested unit with its own identity and extraction process (one crawled website, one PDF, one video, or one code repository), and \emph{knowledgebase} to mean a set of sources a user has grouped together. A \emph{chunk} is the unit actually embedded and retrieved, produced from a source by some modality-specific chunking function. A \emph{query} may target one source, an entire knowledgebase, or an explicit, user-selected subset of sources spanning one or more knowledgebases; we call the last case a \emph{combined-source query}, and it is the case that introduces requirements a single-corpus system does not have.

Three properties distinguish multi-source retrieval from ordinary multi-document retrieval within one corpus:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Heterogeneous extraction.} Each source type has its own extraction pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:modality}) that yields text of different lexical character (crawled prose, PDF-extracted prose with layout artifacts, ASR-transcribed speech, and source code) before any retrieval model sees it.
\item \textbf{Heterogeneous chunking.} As Section~\ref{sec:chunking} discusses, the natural retrieval unit differs by modality (a paragraph, a timestamped transcript segment, a function-scoped code block), so a single global chunking function is a simplification rather than a given.
\item \textbf{Source-level accountability.} A citation in a multi-source system must identify which source, not just which passage, produced a piece of evidence. A combined-source query also carries an implicit expectation that every source the user explicitly selected was actually considered, a requirement with no analogue when the corpus is one undifferentiated collection.
\end{itemize}

The move from sparse lexical matching to learned dense representations began with semantic-matching architectures such as DSSM \cite{huang2013dssm} and matured into dual-encoder retrievers, of which Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) \cite{karpukhin2020dpr} is the canonical instance. In a dual encoder, queries and passages are embedded separately and compared by inner product or cosine similarity. Because the passage side is fixed at index time, its embeddings can be precomputed offline and served through approximate nearest-neighbor search, leaving query-time cost independent of corpus size. For a heterogeneous knowledgebase this property is what makes a single shared index across modalities feasible in the first place: web, PDF, transcript, and code chunks can be encoded by the same model and placed in one searchable space. The same design also carries an assumption worth flagging early, that one encoder's geometry is equally suitable for prose, speech, and code; Sections~\ref{sec:reranking} and \ref{sec:challenges} return to the consequences of that assumption.

Retrieval-augmented generation \cite{lewis2020rag} pairs such a retriever with a generator that conditions its output on the retrieved passages, marginalizing over retrieved evidence instead of depending on parametric memory alone. Fusion-in-Decoder \cite{izacard2021fid} refined the coupling: a sequence-to-sequence model encodes each retrieved passage separately and lets the decoder attend across all of them at once, which scales with the number of passages better than concatenating them into a single encoder pass. The design space around this core is surveyed at length elsewhere \cite{gao2023ragsurvey} and spans what to retrieve, when, how many passages, how to fuse them, and whether to iterate. Its relevance here is specific: once a system ingests more than one kind of source, every one of those choices must be resolved per modality rather than once globally, and that requirement is the throughline of this survey.

Every dense retriever rests on a sentence- or passage-embedding model. Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sbert} made dense retrieval practical by producing embeddings whose cosine geometry is directly usable for ranking and clustering, sidestepping the quadratic cost of scoring each query-passage pair with a full cross-encoder. Distillation pushes the same idea toward deployment: MiniLM \cite{wang2020minilm,wang2020minilmv2} transfers a large teacher's attention behavior into a compact student, conceding a little accuracy for markedly lower latency and memory use. That balance is decisive for interactive, conversational retrieval, as opposed to offline batch indexing, and the distilled checkpoint \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} \cite{minilm_modelcard} has become a common default in open-source RAG tooling. In a multi-source setting, committing to one such encoder is also a decision to represent every modality with weights trained on a single kind of text, an issue taken up in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}.

Two design choices in this foundational layer take on added weight once a system indexes several source types together. One is the approximate nearest-neighbor index, commonly HNSW-graph-based in production vector databases. Its distance metric (cosine, inner product, or Euclidean) is normally treated as an implementation detail orthogonal to retrieval quality, yet it must be fixed once for the entire index when all sources share a single collection. Fixing it presumes that a sentence encoder's embedding geometry behaves comparably whether the text came from prose, transcribed speech, or code, and that presumption is rarely tested directly in existing multi-source systems. The other choice concerns training data. DPR's original recipe \cite{karpukhin2020dpr} draws in-batch and hard negatives from a single corpus's question-passage pairs, so a system that reuses an off-the-shelf encoder inherits whatever negative distribution that corpus happened to reflect. That distribution is unlikely to match the mixture of prose, transcript, and code negatives the system meets at query time. Work that fine-tunes one encoder against a deliberately multi-modal negative distribution appears to be absent from the literature, an open problem revisited in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}.

\section{Reranking and Hybrid Retrieval}
\label{sec:reranking}

Dense retrieval alone systematically underperforms on queries dominated by exact terms such as identifiers, proper nouns, error codes, and function names, which a bi-encoder's semantic similarity underweights relative to a human's judgment. Two broad families of technique address this. One re-scores an initial dense-retrieved candidate list with a more expensive, jointly-encoded model: cross-encoder rerankers \cite{nogueira2019bert} score a query and passage together rather than independently, and late-interaction models such as ColBERT \cite{khattab2020colbert} compute token-level similarity between query and passage representations, keeping much of a cross-encoder's accuracy at lower cost by deferring interaction to a lighter final step. The other family blends dense and lexical signals directly. CLEAR \cite{gao2020clear} is a learned instance, training a dense retriever to capture precisely the residual that a classical lexical scorer such as BM25 \cite{robertson2009bm25} misses; Reciprocal Rank Fusion \cite{cormack2009rrf} is an unweighted instance, merging independently produced ranked lists using only rank position, with no need for calibrated, comparable scores. Concretely, RRF scores a document $d$ as
\[
\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_i(d)},
\]
summed over each ranked list $i$ in which $d$ appears, with $\mathrm{rank}_i(d)$ its position in list $i$ and $k$ a small constant (commonly 60) that damps the influence of any single list's very top ranks; a document absent from a given list simply contributes zero from that list rather than requiring an imputed score. This rank-only property is exactly what makes RRF attractive for fusing across sources with different lexical and semantic characteristics (Section~\ref{sec:modality}): it needs no assumption that a 0.73 semantic score from a prose retriever means the same thing as a 0.73 score from a code retriever, only that each retriever's own internal ranking is locally meaningful.

This blending family suits multi-source retrieval for reasons a single-corpus system never confronts. Its candidate pool is drawn from structurally different sources at once, mixing prose paragraphs, code fragments, and transcript segments whose embeddings and lexical statistics come from extraction and chunking processes with dissimilar characteristics (Section~\ref{sec:chunking}). A rank-based or explicitly weighted fusion tolerates that dissimilarity better than a score premised on one uniform passage distribution. A structural consequence for ranking follows as well: the output must decide not only \emph{which} passages are best but \emph{whether at least one passage from each requested source} reaches the final answer. Ranking therefore becomes a two-stage problem, scoring followed by selection under a source-coverage constraint, in place of scoring and truncating alone.

The cost asymmetry between these two families determines which one a system can afford. A cross-encoder or ColBERT-style pass \cite{nogueira2019bert,khattab2020colbert} runs a neural forward pass for every candidate. That is easy to justify when reranking, say, the top 100 candidates once per query, but grows proportionally more expensive precisely when a multi-source system widens its candidate pool to guarantee per-source representation; the wider fairness-motivated pool inflates the cost of any reranking stage that scales per candidate. A closed-form hybrid score behaves differently. It is computed from quantities retrieval already produced, namely a similarity score, plus a cheap token-set operation, so widening the pool for fairness leaves its asymptotic cost unchanged. The resulting tradeoff, reranking accuracy against reranking cost under a widened pool, is understated in reranking studies that measure cost only against a single-corpus, unwidened baseline.

\section{Chunking Strategies}
\label{sec:chunking}

Retrieval granularity is usually decided before any retrieval model is chosen: documents are split into fixed-size, overlapping chunks, and the chunk, not the document, becomes the unit that is embedded, indexed, and retrieved. Fixed-size chunking with a modest overlap remains the dominant practical choice because it is cheap, deterministic, and works passably across many document types, but its tradeoffs have increasingly been studied directly rather than assumed. Chunk-size sensitivity varies by domain and document structure, as shown for financial reports, where headers, tables, and dense terminology interact with the chosen chunk boundary in ways that affect downstream retrieval quality measurably \cite{jimenoyepes2024chunking}. ``Late chunking'' \cite{gunther2024latechunking} inverts the usual order of operations, encoding a long document with a long-context embedding model first and deriving chunk-level embeddings from token-level representations afterward, so that each chunk's embedding still reflects surrounding document context that a naively-chunked-then-embedded passage would lose. Comparative studies of chunking strategies more broadly \cite{merola2025chunking} suggest that no single strategy dominates across document types, which is precisely the situation a multi-source system is in by construction: a website's prose, a PDF's mixed prose-and-table layout, a video transcript's spoken, punctuation-light stream, and a source code file's line-oriented structure are different enough that a chunking strategy tuned for one is unlikely to be well-tuned for another. In practice, multi-source systems tend to adopt a shared default chunker for prose-like sources and a distinct chunking unit specifically for whichever modality least resembles prose (most often code, by line, or transcript, by time/character band), rather than forcing one chunking function to serve every modality equally.

Chunk overlap deserves separate comment from chunk size, since the two are often conflated as one hyperparameter. Overlap exists specifically to prevent a fact that straddles a chunk boundary from being unrecoverable by either resulting chunk; the practical cost of overlap is redundant storage and redundant embedding computation proportional to the overlap fraction, which is a fixed, easily-budgeted cost, unlike chunk size, whose effect on retrieval quality is corpus- and query-dependent and harder to budget for in advance. A further wrinkle specific to multi-source systems is that overlap's benefit is not uniform across modalities: a transcript chunked by a time/character band, where segment boundaries already align with natural pauses in speech, needs overlap less than dense, boundary-agnostic prose extracted from a PDF's continuous text flow, which suggests, though we know of no study that measures this directly, that a single global overlap parameter, like a single global chunk size, is a simplification a genuinely multi-modal chunking strategy would eventually need to relax.

An alternative to fixed-size chunking altogether is to place chunk boundaries adaptively, at points where adjacent sentences' embeddings diverge beyond some threshold (``semantic chunking''), so that a chunk boundary tends to coincide with an actual topic shift rather than an arbitrary character count; late chunking \cite{gunther2024latechunking} is one instance of this broader adaptive-boundary family, and comparative evaluations \cite{merola2025chunking} generally find adaptive strategies competitive with, but not uniformly better than, well-tuned fixed-size chunking, at the cost of an extra embedding pass over candidate boundaries before the final chunk set is fixed. For a multi-source system, this cost tradeoff is itself modality-dependent: computing sentence-level embeddings to find adaptive boundaries is a comparatively small additional cost for prose, where sentence segmentation is already a solved subproblem, but is considerably less well-defined for source code, where a ``sentence'' has no clear analogue and syntax-aware boundaries (function or class scope) are already a more natural adaptive criterion than embedding divergence.

A different response to the same underlying problem is to stop treating chunk granularity as a single choice at all. RAPTOR \cite{sarthi2024raptor} recursively clusters and summarizes chunks into a tree, so that retrieval can draw on a fine-grained leaf chunk, a paragraph-level summary, or a whole-document summary according to the level of abstraction a query needs; a broad question is answered from a high node in the tree and a specific one from a leaf, without forcing a single chunk size to serve both. The motivation resembles that of the source-overview chunk examined in Section~\ref{sec:casestudy}, though the mechanism differs: both add a synthetic higher-level representation alongside ordinary chunks because broad queries are otherwise poorly served by fine-grained chunking, but RAPTOR builds a full multi-level tree through recursive LLM summarization, a heavier and more general device than a single deterministic overview chunk derived from source statistics without any generative summarization.

\section{Modality-Specific Retrieval Challenges}
\label{sec:modality}

\subsection{Web Documents}
Retrieval over crawled web content inherits two problems that documents-at-rest avoid. Content must be extracted from a live, often JavaScript-rendered page rather than read from a static file, and the crawl frontier must be bounded by domain, depth, or page count to avoid indexing arbitrary, potentially unbounded linked content. Both sit upstream of retrieval yet directly determine what becomes retrievable: a page that fails to render, or that falls outside the crawl's domain or depth limit, is simply absent from the corpus, a silent failure mode a static-document corpus never exhibits. Near-duplicate content is a subtler hazard. Templated navigation, footers, and boilerplate repeated across every page of a site can dominate a naive chunk's lexical signature and dilute the content that made the page worth crawling; without explicit stripping or down-weighting of such boilerplate, a retriever may return several near-identical, low-information chunks from different pages of the same site in place of one genuinely relevant chunk.

\subsection{Video and Transcript QA}
Two features set transcript-based video QA apart. The retrieval unit is inherently temporal: a useful citation names a timestamp range rather than a document title, and benchmarks such as TVQA \cite{lei2018tvqa} enforce exactly this by scoring temporally localized evidence instead of whole-video labels. The text itself is also less clean when it comes from an automatic transcript rather than a human-authored one, carrying transcription errors, missing punctuation, and imprecise sentence boundaries that the crawled or authored text of other sources usually avoids; this bears directly on how far a system should trust a retrieval-confidence score computed uniformly across all of its sources (Section~\ref{sec:confidence}). Frameworks such as LLoVi \cite{zhang2024llovi} handle long-form video QA by converting video to language (captions or transcripts) and then reasoning over that text with a long-context model, the same reduction of video to retrievable text plus timestamps that a transcript-based multi-source system relies on. Chunk size interacts awkwardly with the timestamp-labeling requirement: a short transcript chunk yields a precise citation timestamp but a weak, fragmentary retrieval signal, because spoken language lacks the self-contained sentence structure of written prose, whereas a longer chunk strengthens the signal at the cost of a timestamp that only loosely locates the answer within a multi-minute span.

\subsection{Code-Repository Retrieval}
Code differs from prose in ways that shape both extraction and retrieval. A repository is not a single document but a directory tree of files linked by import and dependency relationships, and a chunk boundary aligned to function or class scope serves retrieval better than an arbitrary character cut, which motivates line- or syntax-aware chunking over the character-budget chunking common for prose. On the retrieval side, semantic code search was an early target for dense methods, exemplified by the CodeSearchNet benchmark \cite{husain2019codesearchnet}, and has since grown into retrieval-augmented code completion that must decide not just what to retrieve but \emph{where in the repository} to look. RepoCoder \cite{zhang2023repocoder} interleaves retrieval and generation so that an initial, imperfect generation drives better subsequent retrieval, while Repoformer \cite{wu2024repoformer} learns when repository-level retrieval is worth its cost and when a model's local context already suffices. The repository's own scaffolding, its README, directory layout, and test and configuration conventions, is itself retrievable signal distinct from any single file's contents. One point receives only partial treatment in the code-retrieval literature: a repository's files are not independent, and an import or call-graph edge between two of them is direct evidence of relatedness that text-similarity retrieval cannot perceive, so a chunk-level retriever blind to the dependency graph discards structure a compiler or static analyzer already has.

\subsection{Multi-Document and Multi-Source Fusion}
Retrieving well from any single source is necessary but not sufficient once a query may be legitimately answered by evidence spread across several sources at once. End-to-end training of a reader and retriever jointly over multiple documents \cite{sachan2021multidoc} treats multi-document evidence aggregation as a first-class training objective rather than an artifact of independently ranking single documents and concatenating the top results. Reciprocal Rank Fusion \cite{cormack2009rrf}, discussed above as a reranking technique, is equally a multi-source fusion technique when its inputs are ranked lists retrieved independently \emph{per source} rather than fields of one corpus. A distinguishing practical requirement of multi-source (as opposed to merely multi-document) retrieval is source-level fairness: a user who explicitly selects several sources to query jointly has an expectation, often implicit, that each selected source was actually considered, which a pure top-$k$-by-score selection does not guarantee if one source's evidence is systematically weaker or sparser than another's. This requirement is orthogonal to, and can be layered on top of, whichever fusion technique is otherwise used: unweighted rank fusion, learned re-weighting, or a joint reader-retriever objective can all be combined with an explicit source-coverage constraint applied after scoring, since the constraint operates on \emph{which source produced each candidate}, a piece of provenance metadata that fusion techniques generally carry through unchanged but rarely act on directly themselves.

\section{Confidence, Calibration, and Abstention}
\label{sec:confidence}

A grounded QA system that always answers, regardless of retrieval quality, will eventually answer confidently from irrelevant or insufficient evidence. Selective prediction under distribution shift \cite{kamath2020selective} frames this as a decision the system should make explicitly --- answer, or abstain --- rather than as an emergent property of whatever the generator happens to produce. Whether a language model's own reported confidence can be trusted for this purpose is itself contested: \cite{kadavath2022knowwhattheyknow} finds that larger models can be reasonably well calibrated on some self-assessment tasks, but hallucination surveys \cite{ji2023hallucination} document broad and persistent failure modes in which fluent, confident-sounding generation is simply wrong, which argues for grounding the abstention decision in retrieval evidence rather than the generator's self-report wherever retrieval evidence is available. Retrieval augmentation has itself been shown to reduce hallucination directly in open-ended generation \cite{shuster2021retrieval}, which further supports gating on retrieval quality specifically, rather than only on downstream generation behavior after the fact.

Two recent systems make the abstention decision itself part of the model's own reasoning rather than an external gate. Self-RAG \cite{asai2024selfrag} trains a single model to emit reflection tokens that decide at generation time whether to retrieve and whether its own output is supported by what was retrieved, folding the answer/abstain decision into generation instead of computing it upstream from a fixed formula. Corrective RAG \cite{yan2024crag} takes a middle path, adding a lightweight retrieval evaluator that labels evidence as correct, incorrect, or ambiguous and triggers a distinct action per label (using the evidence, discarding it in favor of a web search, or blending the two); this is closer to the confidence-gate pattern described here, with a learned classifier in place of a closed-form heuristic. Neither system has been evaluated on a deliberately multi-source, multi-modal corpus of the kind this survey concerns. Both point in the same direction, moving calibration into a learned component, which a multi-source system could in principle apply per source type rather than uniformly.

Multi-source retrieval complicates calibration in a way single-corpus systems never encounter. When sources have systematically different retrieval-quality distributions, for instance the noisier text of an automatic video transcript against the cleaner text of a crawled webpage, a single fixed confidence threshold applied uniformly will be miscalibrated for at least one of them. A system must either normalize confidence per source-type before thresholding, or make an explicit, stated exception for the source types where uniform thresholding is known to misfire.

In practice abstention is not a binary choice. A system can refuse outright, answer with an attached caveat, or ask a clarifying follow-up question in place of either extreme. The middle option, answering with a caveat, weakens the guarantee from ``no unsupported claims reach the user'' to ``unsupported claims are flagged but still delivered.'' That weaker guarantee can be the more defensible one for source types such as noisy transcripts, where a strict refusal threshold would decline a disproportionate, perhaps majority, share of genuinely answerable questions. Which of the three responses to select for a given confidence band remains an underspecified question in the surveyed literature: most selective-prediction work \cite{kamath2020selective} treats abstention as binary and leaves the graded, per-source-type policy of refusing, caveating, or clarifying to individual builders' judgment rather than to any established methodology.

\section{Case Study: SAGE}
\label{sec:casestudy}

SAGE (Semantic Analysis and Generation Engine) ingests four source types, namely crawled multi-page websites, PDF documents, YouTube video transcripts, and GitHub code repositories, through modality-specific extractors that each terminate in a common chunk representation. It embeds all chunks with the same sentence encoder \cite{reimers2019sbert,wang2020minilm} and answers questions against one, several, or all of a user's sources through a single retrieval, reranking, confidence-gating, and generation pipeline. We do not repeat SAGE's full architecture here; instead we use two of its design decisions as concrete instances of ideas introduced earlier in this survey, and note two further points briefly. Table~\ref{tab:casestudy} previews how each of SAGE's four modalities maps back onto the modality-specific challenges discussed in Section~\ref{sec:modality}.

\textbf{Overview chunks as a chunking-strategy instance.} Section~\ref{sec:chunking} noted that no single chunking strategy dominates across the modalities a multi-source system ingests, and that broad, non-specific queries pose a particular retrieval problem: a question like ``what is this about'' has poor lexical and semantic overlap with any single content chunk, because the answer lives in a source's overall structure rather than any one passage. SAGE addresses this without tuning the chunk size at all. It adds a synthetic, deterministic \emph{overview chunk} to every source at ingestion time, built from the source's own statistics and excerpts (crawled page titles, transcript timestamps, or, for a GitHub repository, a live-introspected on-disk directory tree and detected project conventions), with no additional generative model call. In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:chunking} this is a chunking-strategy decision rather than a generation-time summarization feature: it is an extra chunk added to every source purely to change what is retrievable, and it needs no labeled training data or model fine-tuning to extend to a new modality.

\textbf{The diversity floor as a multi-source-fusion instance.} Section~\ref{sec:modality} identified source-level fairness as a requirement specific to multi-source (not merely multi-document) retrieval: a query that explicitly names several sources should not silently omit one of them from its evidence because that source's single best-matching chunk scored lower than another source's several good chunks. SAGE addresses this requirement with a diversity floor. Before a fixed number of citation slots is filled by reranked score, each explicitly combined source has its single best-scoring chunk reserved, and only the remaining slots are then filled by score across all sources. The mechanism is a direct instance of the score-then-select-under-a-coverage-constraint pattern anticipated in Sections~\ref{sec:reranking} and \ref{sec:modality}. In the reference implementation, a live run confirmed the behavior beyond unit tests: a query spanning a website and a topically unrelated code repository still cited both, and a three-source query produced a cross-source answer that cited each source rather than dropping the weakest-scoring one. The relevance to this survey is that source-level fairness failures look benign when a reranker is examined alone and become visible only under genuinely combined, multi-source queries.

\textbf{Confidence gating with a stated per-modality exception.} Consistent with the calibration concern raised in Section~\ref{sec:confidence}, SAGE computes a single retrieval-derived confidence score from its top-ranked and top-three blended scores plus a small evidence-count bonus, and refuses to answer in grounded mode below a fixed threshold. Video sources are the one exception: chunks originating from them are exempted from hard refusal and instead receive a lower-confidence warning, on the stated reasoning that automatically transcribed text is measurably noisier than the system's cleaner-text sources. This realizes the explicit per-source exception anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:confidence}, applied to a single modality rather than derived from a general per-source calibration procedure.

\textbf{Evidence, not a benchmark.} SAGE's test suite (95 test functions across 12 files, covering chunking, URL normalization, reranker and confidence formula sanity checks, per-modality overview-chunk generation, and an end-to-end API integration suite) shows that the mechanisms above are implemented and exercised. It is not a quantitative retrieval-quality or answer-quality result. SAGE reports no benchmark score against a labeled dataset, and none is constructed here, since a combined-modality benchmark able to support such a score does not yet exist (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).

\textbf{Minor points.} Two aspects of SAGE that fall outside the survey's focus warrant a sentence each. Because the crawler follows redirects, its server-side request forgery guard re-checks the resolved address at every hop rather than only at the entry URL \cite{owasp2021ssrf}; and because retrieved web text is untrusted, the generator is instructed to treat that text as quotable material rather than as commands, limiting indirect prompt injection \cite{greshake2023promptinjection}.

At the product level, SAGE sits between two familiar categories: consumer ``chat with your sources'' tools such as NotebookLM \cite{notebooklm2023}, and composability frameworks such as LangChain \cite{langchain} and LlamaIndex \cite{llamaindex}. It matches neither, being an opinionated multi-source pipeline rather than a finished single-user application or a general library. \section{Open Challenges}
\label{sec:challenges}

Several problems recur across the surveyed systems and papers without a settled answer.

\emph{Cross-modal fusion at the scoring level} remains largely heuristic. Blended semantic-lexical scores, rank fusion, and learned combination methods (Section~\ref{sec:reranking}) each rest on different and usually untested assumptions about whether a score from one modality's chunk is comparable in magnitude to a score from another's. SAGE's diversity floor avoids the question by guaranteeing representation instead of asserting cross-modality score comparability, which yields a fairness guarantee at the expense of a true relevance ordering across sources.

\emph{Confidence calibration across heterogeneous corpora} is equally unsettled. Per-source-type exceptions, such as the video-transcript exception in Section~\ref{sec:casestudy}, are practical patches rather than calibrated solutions; no published method calibrates a single retrieval-confidence score jointly across genuinely heterogeneous modalities using one labeled dataset that spans all of them.

\emph{Incremental re-indexing} has received little attention in the multi-source case. Chunking strategies (Section~\ref{sec:chunking}) are typically presented as one-time operations, yet a source that changes over time, such as a recrawled website or an updated repository, forces a decision about which existing chunks to preserve and which to re-embed. Content-addressed chunk identifiers can make that cost/correctness tradeoff explicit but do not resolve it.

\emph{Evaluation} for multi-source, multi-modal RAG trails single-corpus evaluation. Most retrieval and QA benchmarks, including several in Section~\ref{sec:modality}, are modality-specific, and there is no widely adopted benchmark that jointly measures retrieval quality, citation correctness, and confidence calibration across web, document, video, and code sources combined in one query; such a benchmark is what would allow systems like SAGE to be compared on equal terms rather than by architecture description alone. Standard metrics widen the gap rather than close it. Recall@$k$, MRR, and nDCG are each defined against a single fixed relevance judgment per query, presuming a labeled query-document pair drawn from one corpus. Extending them to a combined-source query first requires deciding whether relevance judgments are pooled across sources or required per source, the latter capturing the fairness property of Sections~\ref{sec:modality} and \ref{sec:casestudy}, and no standard convention yet governs that choice.

\emph{Fine-tuning against a multi-modal negative distribution}, raised in Section~\ref{sec:foundations}, is a training-side gap rather than an architectural one. Nearly every widely used open-source sentence encoder, including the one in our case study, was trained on question-passage pairs from a single kind of corpus. Published work that deliberately constructs hard negatives spanning prose, transcript, and code together, to fine-tune one encoder for genuinely mixed-modality retrieval, appears to be missing; the common alternative is to fine-tune separate encoders per modality and reconcile their outputs afterward.

\emph{Dependency-aware retrieval for structured sources}, raised in Section~\ref{sec:modality}'s discussion of code, generalizes beyond code: a website's internal link graph and a PDF's cross-references are analogous structural signals that most chunk-level dense retrievers discard identically to how they discard a repository's import graph, suggesting that the code-specific graph-aware retrieval methods surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:modality} may be a more general opportunity for structured, multi-source retrieval than their code-specific framing in the literature suggests. GraphRAG \cite{edge2024graphrag} is a concrete instance of taking structure this seriously within a single corpus: it has an LLM extract an entity-relationship graph from the source text first, clusters that graph into communities, and pre-summarizes each community so that broad, corpus-level questions can be answered from community summaries rather than from any single retrieved chunk. It is, notably, built and evaluated for one homogeneous text corpus rather than heterogeneous multi-source input; extending an LLM-extracted entity graph across genuinely different modalities (linking a code symbol to the paragraph of a PDF that documents it, for instance) remains open, and would be a natural, if expensive, way to make the cross-source relationships this survey discusses explicit rather than left to a retriever to rediscover implicitly at query time.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Grounded question answering over heterogeneous, multi-source knowledgebases relies on the same core techniques as single-corpus RAG: dense retrieval, hybrid reranking, chunking, and confidence-gated generation. Each, however, must be reconsidered once the corpus is assembled from structurally different sources at query time. Chunking must accommodate prose, transcripts, and code without assuming one size fits all. Reranking must be paired with an explicit source-fairness mechanism as soon as queries can name several sources at once. Confidence estimation must either normalize across source-type-specific quality distributions or state its exceptions explicitly. SAGE's overview-chunk mechanism and diversity floor served as checkable instances of the latter two adjustments, illustrating how one system resolves tradeoffs the broader literature leaves open.

Four problems stand out as most in need of dedicated attention as multi-source RAG systems mature: cross-modal score comparability, joint confidence calibration, incremental re-indexing, and combined-modality evaluation. The highest-leverage next steps follow directly. A standardized multi-modal benchmark should score retrieval quality, citation correctness, and confidence calibration across web, document, video, and code sources together rather than one at a time. Retrieval confidence should be calibrated jointly across heterogeneous corpora instead of patched per source type. Retrieval and reranking pipelines should choose their chunking and fusion strategy adaptively by source modality rather than applying one global configuration to every ingested source.
